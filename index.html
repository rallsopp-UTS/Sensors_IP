Track Chosen: Track B vision-guided pick. UR3 in MuJoCo with a wrist-mounted RGB-D camera. Detect a target object on a tabletop, plan an approach, and execute a pick using IBVS or PBVS. Required: camera calibration; depth-based object localisation; state-space joint controller; observer for unmeasured states. Constraint: object position is randomised between trials and the system must demonstrate robustness. Own project. Must include, at minimum, a state-space model with controllability and observability analysis, at least one calibrated exteroceptive sensor, a Kalman filter or observer, and a feedback controller running on estimated state.

Part A information

Equations

1. Servo Torque

τ= Kp(u−q) − Kvq˙

The motors are position servos. u is the position command, and Kp and Kv come from the XML.

2. Arm Dynamics

M(q)q¨ + g(q) = Kp(u−q) − Kvq˙

The motor’s force needs to be enough to move the arm and to hold its weight against the force of gravity.

3. Holding Command

At rest, q¨=0 and q˙=0, so the servo torque must cancel gravity:

Kp(u0−q0) = g(q0) → u0=q0 + Kp−1 g(q0)

4. Linearisation

M0δq¨ = −(Kp+G0)δq − Kvδq˙ + Kpδu

For small movements the arm’s speed changes because of the motor spring, the brake and your new command.

5. State-Space Form

x˙ =Ax+Bδu y=Cx
A= [ 0 I −M0−1(Kp+G0) −M0−1Kv ]
B= [ 0 M0−1Kp ]
C= [ I0 ]

A says how the arm’s state changes by itself, B says how your commands change it, and C says what the sensors can see.

6. Controllability and Observability

Controllability Matrix= [ B AB A2B ⋯ A11B ]
Observability Matrix= [ C CA CA2 ⋮ CA11 ]

If both rank tests give 12 out of 12, your commands can move the arm into any state, and the angle sensors are enough to work out every state, including speeds.

Assumptions

  1. Only small movements. The model is a straight-line approximation, so it is only accurate when the arm stays close to the starting pose.
  2. The arm starts at rest. The model is built around the arm sitting still at the key frame, held up by the command u0.
  3. The motors behave like a simple spring and brake. The stiffness (Kp) and braking (Kv) numbers are fixed and come straight from the XML.
  4. The swinging-out forces are ignored. They are tiny when the arm moves slowly, so they are left out.
  5. Nothing touches anything. The arm doesn’t hit the table, the cube or itself.
  6. The green cube is left out. As we were only told to pick the cube, it is unnecessary for us to account for the weight of the cube when the arm is moving.
  7. The motors never hit their limits. Their maximum force and command range are ignored, which is fine for small movements.
  8. There is no extra friction or damping. The XML only gives the joints a small spin weight and no other drag, so the motor brake is the only slowing force.
  9. MuJoCo’s numbers are right. The mass matrix and the gravity are calculated by MuJoCo, so any small error there carries into the model.
  10. Time is continuous. The model treats time as smooth, and a separate step turns it into a version that works in small time steps.
  11. The camera is not part of the model. It only describes the arm. The camera and the marker belong to the sensing part.

Part C: Estimation

A Kalman filter for the UR3 joint state. Anything highlighted like this still needs filling in before you submit.

1. What is estimated, and why

The controller (Part D) and the camera frame chain (Part B) need the arm’s joint angles and speeds, but the only sensors are noisy joint encoders, which measure angles only. The estimator therefore has to clean up the noisy angle readings, and estimate the six joint speeds, which no sensor measures.

Statex = [q − q₀ ; q̇], 12 states: 6 joint angles and 6 joint speeds, as deviations from the keyframe pose q₀
Inputu = ctrl − u₀, the position-actuator command relative to the command u₀ that holds the arm still at q₀
Measurementy = q − q₀ + noise, the 6 encoder readings

The controller uses only the estimate x̂. Simulator ground truth is read in two places only: ReadEncoders(), as the simulated sensor with noise added, and the logging and evaluation code (EvaluateEstimator, ReplayKF, EstimateQ), where it is used to score the filter and is never fed back.

2. Model and observability

The model is the linearisation of the UR3 at the keyframe, built in StateSpaceModel:

ẋ = A x + B u        y = C x

A = [ 0              I          ]      B = [ 0       ]      C = [ I  0 ]
    [ −M⁻¹(Kp + G₀)  −M⁻¹ Kv   ]          [ M⁻¹ Kp  ]

Here M is the arm mass matrix, G₀ the gravity Jacobian, and Kp and Kv the servo stiffness and damping. It is discretised with a zero-order hold at the simulator timestep, Ts = timestep s.

Observability: the observability matrix has rank 12 of 12 (scaled test; the unscaled rank test is numerically unreliable because A has a very large dynamic range). All 12 states, including the unmeasured speeds, can therefore be recovered from the encoder angles through the model.

3. Filter design

A standard discrete Kalman filter, using the Joseph form for the covariance update, which keeps P symmetric and positive definite.

Predict:  x̂⁻ = A_d x̂ + B_d u          P⁻ = A_d P A_dᵀ + Q
Update:   S = C P⁻ Cᵀ + R             K = P⁻ Cᵀ S⁻¹
          x̂ = x̂⁻ + K (y − C x̂⁻)
          P = (I − KC) P⁻ (I − KC)ᵀ + K R Kᵀ

The filter runs inside the pick loop at every simulation step (KFStep). Its output feeds the LQR controller and the PBVS frame chain.

Acknowledgement. The filter structure, the observability check, the Q/R sweep and the consistency checks follow the Week 7 tutorial (Observers and the Kalman Filter), adapted here to the UR3 model.

4. Choice of R and Q

R: measurement noise

R = σ²I with σ = 0.002 rad, the standard deviation of the noise the simulator adds to the encoder readings (ReadEncoders). This is an assumed sensor specification. The NIS sweep in section 6 confirms the filter is consistent with it: NIS is about 6 (the expected value for 6 measurements) only at the nominal R.

Q: process noise

Q represents how wrong the linear model’s one-step prediction can be. It was set in two stages.

Shape from data. Along a logged pick run, the one-step prediction error of the linear model against the simulator was measured for each state (EstimateQ). Using logged truth to tune Q offline is standard practice; the filter never sees truth while it runs. Measured model error (RMS per step, arm with gripper):

One-step model error per joint
Joint123456
Angle error (rad)4.6e-72.6e-61.4e-62.4e-73.1e-76.6e-8
Speed error (rad/s)3.7e-42.3e-31.1e-31.3e-42.4e-41.2e-4

The model error differs a lot between joints, about 20× between the largest and smallest speed error, so a single Q for all joints was a poor fit.

Scale from consistency. The one-step errors alone gave a badly overconfident filter (NEES about 1780 against an expected 12). One overall scale factor on the variances, final multiplier, was chosen from the NEES sweep so that NEES is about 12. A likely reason a large factor is needed is that the model error is persistent rather than random from step to step, so it accumulates over many steps. This is a hypothesis; it was not tested separately.

An earlier hand-tuned Q gave similar accuracy (NEES 11.9, position RMSE 0.00029 rad), but its scale factors had no stated origin, so it was replaced.

The speed estimate, which no sensor provides, is accurate to about 3 mrad/s. NEES and NIS both match their expected values, so the filter’s reported uncertainty matches its real error.

What the sweeps show:

  • Q trades consistency against accuracy. Raising Q makes the filter more cautious (NEES falls from 15 to below 4) but the speed estimate gets much noisier (0.0028 to 0.034 rad/s from ×1 to ×100). Lowering Q does the reverse. The chosen Q is the point where the reported uncertainty matches the real error.
  • NIS identifies the right R. NIS is 6.0 only at the nominal R and drops towards 0 as R is inflated. Larger R also smooths the speed estimate (0.0028 to 0.0007 rad/s) but makes position worse (0.0002 to 0.00055 rad), because the filter stops trusting the encoders.
  • If R were 10× smaller, the filter would trust the encoders more: higher gain and an estimate that follows sensor noise more closely. Earlier sweeps on this filter, before the gripper was added, showed NIS rising to about 58 at R × 0.1, meaning the filter becomes overconfident about the sensor.
  • NIS alone cannot detect an overconfident Q. NIS stayed near 6 for every Q tried, including settings with NEES in the thousands. NEES, which needs truth, is what exposes it.

7. Failure analysis: the gripper changed the model

When the gripper was added to the scene, the filter became overconfident without any change to the estimator code.

The added mass at the wrist changed which joints have the largest model error: the largest speed error moved from the elbow to the shoulder lift, and joint 5’s grew about 5×. Q measured on the old arm no longer fitted. NEES was the diagnostic that caught it, and re-running EstimateQ on the new arm fixed it.

Separately, during integration a merge dropped the Q scale factor, which pushed NEES to about 4900 until it was found and restored. Both cases are why the consistency check is run after every model or code change.

8. Limitations

What the results do and do not support:

  • Linearised model. The model is valid near the keyframe. The numbers above are for the pick trajectory used here. A pick that moves the arm much further from the keyframe would degrade accuracy and would call for re-linearising along the path or an EKF.
  • One run, one noise sequence. Q was measured and scored on the same logged run (noise seed 0), and only encoder noise was varied in the sweeps. The results show consistency on this run. They do not yet show it holds across other noise realisations or object positions. Replace with repeated-trial results if you complete them.
  • Assumed noise. The 0.002 rad encoder noise is the simulator’s own injected noise, so NIS verifies the filter against a known noise level, not against a real sensor.
  • Speed error floor. The speed accuracy is limited by the model error, not by sensor noise: raising R improves it but costs position accuracy.
