Part A information

Equations

1. Servo Torque

τ= Kp(u−q) − Kvq˙

The motors are position servos. u is the position command, and Kp and Kv come from the XML.

2. Arm Dynamics

M(q)q¨ + g(q) = Kp(u−q) − Kvq˙

The motor’s force needs to be enough to move the arm and to hold its weight against the force of gravity.

3. Holding Command

At rest, q¨=0 and q˙=0, so the servo torque must cancel gravity:

Kp(u0−q0) = g(q0) → u0=q0 + Kp−1 g(q0)

4. Linearisation

M0δq¨ = −(Kp+G0)δq − Kvδq˙ + Kpδu

For small movements the arm’s speed changes because of the motor spring, the brake and your new command.

5. State-Space Form

x˙ =Ax+Bδu y=Cx
A= [ 0 I −M0−1(Kp+G0) −M0−1Kv ]
B= [ 0 M0−1Kp ]
C= [ I0 ]

A says how the arm’s state changes by itself, B says how your commands change it, and C says what the sensors can see.

6. Controllability and Observability

Controllability Matrix= [ B AB A2B ⋯ A11B ]
Observability Matrix= [ C CA CA2 ⋮ CA11 ]

If both rank tests give 12 out of 12, your commands can move the arm into any state, and the angle sensors are enough to work out every state, including speeds.

Assumptions

  1. Only small movements. The model is a straight-line approximation, so it is only accurate when the arm stays close to the starting pose.
  2. The arm starts at rest. The model is built around the arm sitting still at the key frame, held up by the command u0.
  3. The motors behave like a simple spring and brake. The stiffness (Kp) and braking (Kv) numbers are fixed and come straight from the XML.
  4. The swinging-out forces are ignored. They are tiny when the arm moves slowly, so they are left out.
  5. Nothing touches anything. The arm doesn’t hit the table, the cube or itself.
  6. The green cube is left out. As we were only told to pick the cube, it is unnecessary for us to account for the weight of the cube when the arm is moving.
  7. The motors never hit their limits. Their maximum force and command range are ignored, which is fine for small movements.
  8. There is no extra friction or damping. The XML only gives the joints a small spin weight and no other drag, so the motor brake is the only slowing force.
  9. MuJoCo’s numbers are right. The mass matrix and the gravity are calculated by MuJoCo, so any small error there carries into the model.
  10. Time is continuous. The model treats time as smooth, and a separate step turns it into a version that works in small time steps.
  11. The camera is not part of the model. It only describes the arm. The camera and the marker belong to the sensing part.

Structural Reasoning for Full Rank

Controllability

Take the first 2 block columns of the controllability matrix, B and AB. The 12×12 matrix has zeros in the top left, and both off-diagonal blocks are M0−1Kp. A matrix of that shape is full rank whenever M0−1Kp can be inverted. It can, for 2 reasons.

  • M0−1 is the inverse of M0, which comes from the DfDa line. A mass matrix is positive definite, since every joint has inertia plus the armature term, so it can be inverted.
  • Kp is diagonal with positive entries, so it can be inverted too.

Each command pushes its own joint acceleration, and the identity block I in the top row of A carries that through into the joint angles, so no direction is out of reach.

Observability

The C matrix is [I,0], and multiplying it by A gives [0,I]. Stacked together, these form an identity matrix, which has rank 12 trivially. In plain words, the encoders read the angles directly, and the speeds are how fast those angles change, so the speeds can be recovered.

G0 never appears in [B,AB], and Kv only sits in the bottom-right block, which doesn’t change the rank. So the full-rank result holds at any pose and with any damping, not just the key frame. It would only break if a Kp entry were zero or if C lost an encoder.

Eigenvalues of A

What they mean

  1. The eigenvalues of A are all negative, so the system is stable and after any small knock the arm would come back to rest.
  2. The values are real, which means there is no wobble. The arm creeps to the target without overshooting because the motor brakes are stronger than the springs.
  3. The slower values (−5.00 to −5.09) are set by the motor settings. Each fades in about 0.2 s, so the arm takes roughly 0.8 s to settle after a command.
  4. The faster values (−178 to −926) are the brakes killing the speed quickly, with each fading in about 1 to 6 ms.
  5. The large gap between the slow and fast values makes the system stiff, with a ratio of about 180 to 1.

Why it matters to this project

  1. Stability → The arm is stable with no controller at all, so Part D’s job is tracking, not stabilising.
  2. Speed limit → The arm is not able to follow a command faster than 0.2 seconds.
  3. Sample period → The fastest mode limits forward Euler to a step below about 2 ms, which is why we discretised with a zero-order hold.
  4. Estimator → The observer should settle faster than the arm moves, so we aimed well above 5 per second.

Part C: Estimation

1. What the filter does

The arm only has encoders, and they measure joint angles with some noise. The controller also needs joint speeds, which nothing measures. The Kalman filter takes the noisy angles and gives a cleaner estimate of both the angles and the speeds.

State6 joint angles and 6 joint speeds (12 states), measured as the change from the starting pose
InputThe command sent to the motors, relative to the command that holds the arm still
MeasurementThe 6 encoder angles, plus noise

The controller only uses the filter’s estimate. Simulator ground truth is used in two places: to create the noisy encoder readings (ReadEncoders()), and to score the filter afterwards (EvaluateEstimator, ReplayKF, EstimateQ). It is never fed back into the filter or controller.

2. The model and observability

The filter predicts using the arm’s state-space model, which is built at the starting pose in StateSpaceModel (see Part A) and converted to 0.002 s steps, the same as the simulator.

Observability: the rank is 12 of 12. This means all 12 states, including the speeds, can be worked out from the angle readings alone, because the model links each speed to how its angle changes.

3. How the filter works

Every 0.002 s it does two steps:

  1. Predict: use the model and the last command to guess the new state.
  2. Correct: compare the guess to the encoder reading and move the guess towards it.
Predict:  x̂⁻ = A_d x̂ + B_d u          P⁻ = A_d P A_dᵀ + Q
Update:   S = C P⁻ Cᵀ + R             K = P⁻ Cᵀ S⁻¹
          x̂ = x̂⁻ + K (y − C x̂⁻)
          P = (I − KC) P⁻ (I − KC)ᵀ + K R Kᵀ

Here x̂ is the estimate and P is how uncertain the filter is. The last line is the “Joseph form”, which keeps P valid. This is a linear Kalman filter built at one pose, not an EKF. The filter runs inside the pick loop (KFStep), and its output goes to the LQR controller and the PBVS step.

Source. The filter structure, the observability check, the Q and R sweeps and the NEES and NIS checks follow the Week 7 tutorial, adapted to the UR3 model.

4. Where R and Q come from

R: how noisy the encoders are

R is the encoder noise variance, 0.002² rad². This is the same noise the simulator adds in ReadEncoders, so R matches by design. Section 6 confirms it: NIS is about 6 only at this value.

Q: how much to distrust the model

Q is set in two steps.

Step 1: measure the model error. On a logged run, we measured how far the linear model’s one-step prediction was from the simulator for each state (EstimateQ). Using truth to tune Q offline is allowed, because the running filter never sees it. The error is different for each joint, so each joint gets its own Q value.

The raw one-step error underestimates what the filter needs, so a scale is applied and checked with NEES.

5. Results

One 20 s closed-loop run, with PBVS and LQR driving the arm from the estimate. The first 500 samples were skipped as start-up. Truth was used only for scoring.

The angle estimate is about 8 times more accurate than a raw encoder reading. The speed estimate is accurate to about 4 mrad/s even though no sensor measures it. NEES and NIS are both at their expected values, so the filter’s stated uncertainty matches its real error. Coverage is slightly below 0.997, so the filter is a little overconfident on some state.

What NEES and NIS mean. NEES checks the filter’s uncertainty against its real error over all 12 states (it needs truth). NIS checks the filter’s prediction of each encoder reading against what the encoder really said (it does not need truth).

  • Q is a trade-off. A smaller Q gives a slightly better speed error, but the filter becomes overconfident (NEES 160). A larger Q is safe but slow: the speed error is more than three times worse at ×10. The chosen Q has NEES at 12 and the lowest angle error.
  • NIS tells you if R is right. NIS is 6.0 at the real noise level and changes a lot when R is wrong (58.5 at ×0.1, 0.6 at ×10).
  • If R were 10 times smaller, the filter would trust the encoders too much. NIS rises to 58.5, the speed error rises from 0.0040 to 0.0136 rad/s, and coverage falls to 0.915.
  • NIS cannot spot a bad Q. It stayed between 5.9 and 6.2 for every Q, even when NEES was 160. Only NEES, which needs truth, showed the problem.

7. A failure we found: the gripper changed the model

When the gripper was added, the filter became overconfident even though we did not change the filter code.

The gripper’s extra weight at the wrist changed which joints have the biggest model error. The biggest speed error moved from the elbow to the shoulder lift, and joint 5’s error grew about 5 times. The old Q no longer fitted. NEES caught the problem, and re-running EstimateQ on the new arm fixed it.

In a separate case, a code merge dropped the Q scale factor and NEES jumped to about 4900 until it was found and restored. This is why we check NEES after every model or code change.

8. Limitations

  • One pose. The model is only accurate near the starting pose. A pick that moves the arm much further would make the estimates worse, and would need a model that is updated along the path, or an EKF.
  • One run. Q was measured, tuned and scored on the same run, and the sweeps replay that run. This shows the filter is consistent here, but not yet that it holds for other noise or object positions.
  • Truth used for tuning. Q was measured offline using simulator truth. The running filter never sees truth.
  • Simulated noise. The encoder noise is the simulator’s own, so NIS checks the filter against a known noise level, not a real sensor.
  • Speed accuracy. It is limited by the model error, not the sensor noise. Raising R improves speed but makes the angle worse.
